misplaced efforts

october 4, 2026

as a new school year begins i get a lot of incoming freshmen who knew me in high school reaching back out and asking for advice on being successful in college, or at least how to set themselves up for as high a chance for success as possible. as nice as it is to see that they're entering college so locked in and with what appears to be very clear direction, i cannot help but feel that it can't be very worthwhile to be spending so much time and thought on things like internships so early on, especially when so much of it is out of your control. i guess there are really two axes that i'm talking about here.

one axis is that I see a lot of people around me spending a lot of time in areas that they think will yield high returns, when it could be spent on much more valuable areas that they actually possess some drive for. for example, I see many underclassmen CS majors putting all their time, effort and stress into making sure they're top of their classes, getting As and not A-s, and then assuming that the 4.0 on their resume will translate to anything (this doesn't apply to people seeing PhDs). for this I blame the college application system, because it conditioned students to overindex on their GPA. it is tough, then, to later see the same students later apply to hundreds of software engineering internships, land almost no interviews, and wonder where the fruit of their efforts had gone. when they ask how this happened, the hard answer is that they have perfectly molded themselves to the exact blueprint of CS student that every other resume looks like. i have found this to be especially true because, having applied to highly competitive CS-major-targeted roles as a physics major, it's clear that spending your efforts into a crafting an unexpected background with targeted projects and a compelling story (or just any semblance of differentiating yourself) gets much more traction with recruiting. another example: several people have asked me for help securing lab positions, and months later when I ask how it's going, i find out they don't enjoy research at all, are not publishing anytime soon, and were motivated by the vague notion of "i should have research on my resume." i don't have the heart to tell them that without some meaningful publication or interesting problems to speak of, they probably wasted their time. i would describe this as putting your eggs in the wrong basket.

another axis of misplaced effort is worrying about getting into places where it wouldn't even make sense for you to be. i see this in many places, for example: middling sophomores wondering why citadel didn't give them an interview, or people who undeniably slacked off in high school then stressing about getting their MIT, stanford, and ivy league essays written. i think that this is because a lot of social media and selection bias with people posting positive results has created the popular wisdom that you should "larp" until you make it, even when the case is so extreme as to be an absurd moonshot. obviously this issue is exacerbated by AI - it's baffling to me sometimes when I meet somebody who is disappointed that a top-tier quant firm didn't want them, but then I find out they can't even describe their own projects because claude one-shotted them. like they don't realize they should be infinitely more surprised if somehow the stars perfectly misaligned to give them an offer. before signaling bafflement, people should stop and consider: if you did land the role, would you even thrive there? some people now seem to believe that the effort it takes to get into a highly competitive company/school lies in the application process itself, and forget that the application is not the only prerequisite to the role - you must first know your shit and be well-suited for the role. it should not be surprising that you'd fail to reap what you didn't sow.

for me, the takeaway (and what I tell the freshmen) is to keep your head down, don't listen to the noise, and grind the things that actually have signal. i'm sure that the most cracked people at any school are definitely not going around being loud about how successful they are in school and recruiting - they're too busy being on the other end of the bell curve.
